# Abstract

## Original Text (Verbatim)

How many words are needed to define all the words in a dictionary? Graph-theoretic analysis reveals that about 10% of a dictionary is a unique Kernel of words that define one another and all the rest, but this is not the smallest such subset. The Kernel consists of one huge strongly connected component (SCC), about half its size, the Core, surrounded by many small SCCs, the Satellites. Core words can define one another but not the rest of the dictionary. The Kernel also contains many overlapping Minimal Grounding Sets (MGSs), each about the same size as the Core, each part-Core, part-Satellites. Any one of them can define all the rest of the dictionary, are learned earlier, more frequent and more frequent than the rest of the dictionary. Satellite words, not correlated with age or frequency, are less concrete (more abstract) words that are also needed for full lexical power.

---

## Our Interpretation

This paper adds functional differentiation to the earlier kernel story. It says the hidden structure is not just there mathematically; its parts may correspond to different kinds of lexical work, especially the balance between early central vocabulary and the extra, sometimes more abstract, words needed for full expressive coverage.
