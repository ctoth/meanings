# Hidden Structure and Function in the Lexicon

## One-Sentence Summary
This paper refines the earlier graph-theoretic dictionary program by separating the `Kernel`, `Core`, `Satellites`, and `Minimal Grounding Sets (MGSs)` and arguing that these hidden graph components have different psycholinguistic roles, with Core words especially early/frequent and Satellite words supplying additional abstract lexical power needed for full definitional coverage. *(p.1-11)*

## Problem Addressed
The paper asks not only how many words must be grounded for the rest of the dictionary to be learnable by definition, but also how the words in different hidden components of dictionary graphs differ from the rest of the lexicon. *(p.1-3)*

## Key Contributions
- Distinguishes clearly among the full Dictionary `D`, the `Kernel K`, the `Core C`, the `Satellites S`, and overlapping `MGSs`, emphasizing that these objects are not interchangeable. *(p.1-3)*
- States that the Kernel is about 10% of a dictionary, the Core is the largest SCC inside the Kernel, Satellites are the smaller SCCs around it, and any MGS is roughly half the size of the Kernel. *(p.1-3, p.7)*
- Reports psycholinguistic differences using MRC features: words in hidden structures are learned younger, are more frequent, and are more imageable/concrete than words outside the Kernel. *(p.3-5)*
- Argues that once correlations among psycholinguistic variables are controlled, the distinctive contribution of the Satellite layer becomes more visible: these are words additionally needed for expressive power and they can be more abstract than Core words. *(p.3-6)*
- Extends the analysis to mini-dictionaries generated by an online dictionary game in order to inspect individual words rather than only large dictionary aggregates. *(p.7-9)*

## Study Design (empirical papers)
- **Type:** Graph-theoretic lexicon analysis combined with psycholinguistic variable comparisons and mini-dictionary experiments. *(p.1-9)*
- **Population:** Four natural-language dictionaries: LDOCE, CIDE, Merriam-Webster, and WordNet, plus smaller mini-dictionaries from an online dictionary game. *(p.7-9)*
- **Intervention(s):** Kernel/Core/Satellite/MGS decomposition plus ANOVAs and multiple regression over MRC psycholinguistic variables. *(p.3-5)*
- **Comparator(s):** Dictionary vs Kernel vs Core vs Satellites vs MGS. *(p.2-5)*
- **Primary endpoint(s):** Whether hidden graph structures differ psycholinguistically and what functional role each component may play. *(p.3-6)*
- **Secondary endpoint(s):** Whether mini-dictionaries exhibit analogous hidden structure. *(p.7-9)*
- **Follow-up:** N/A. *(p.1-11)*

## Methodology
The paper inherits the graph objects introduced in earlier work: the dictionary graph, its Kernel, its SCC decomposition, and MGSs as cycle-breaking seed sets. It then compares these components using MRC variables such as age of acquisition, frequency, imageability, and concreteness. Pairwise multiple regression is used to separate the effect of correlated psycholinguistic factors. The paper also analyzes mini-dictionaries generated in an online game, which contain around 37-50 words and can be fully inspected word by word. *(p.2-5, p.7-9)*

## Key Equations / Statistical Models
None in formal equation notation. The paper reports ANOVAs and pairwise multiple regression comparisons over psycholinguistic variables. *(p.3-5)*

## Methods & Implementation Details
- The Core is defined as the union of the Kernel's SCCs that receive no incoming definitional links from outside themselves; in full dictionaries this is usually one large SCC. *(p.2, p.10 n.2)*
- MGSs overlap and are numerous; each is a subset of the Kernel and each grounds the entire dictionary. *(p.1-3)*
- Psycholinguistic comparisons suggest a rough ordering `MGS > C > S > K > D` when looking at raw trends for younger acquisition, higher frequency, and greater imageability/concreteness, but this picture changes once correlations are factored out. *(p.3-5)*
- After pairwise regression, age is the dominant explanatory factor; frequency and imageability contribute less, and the interesting reversals concentrate in the Satellite layer. *(p.3-5)*
- The paper argues that Satellite words may be less concrete and more abstract because they are the extra words needed, beyond Core words, to construct MGSs and thereby define the rest of the lexicon. *(p.5-6)*
- Mini-dictionaries generated by the online game display the same gross hidden structure, but in some mini-dictionaries the Core is not a single SCC and the MGS/Core relation can differ from full dictionaries. *(p.8-11)*

## Results Summary
The paper confirms that dictionary graphs have a stable hidden organization rather than a single undifferentiated kernel. The Kernel is small, the Core is central but not sufficient to define the whole dictionary, the Satellites and MGSs are essential for full grounding power, and these components differ psycholinguistically. Raw trends suggest that words become younger, more frequent, and more concrete as one moves inward, but the finer picture is subtler: Satellite words may carry the more abstract lexical work needed beyond the early-learned Core. *(p.2-7)*

## Limitations
- The study relies on correlations among MRC psycholinguistic variables that are themselves highly intercorrelated. *(p.3-5)*
- MGS computation is only reported fully for the two smaller dictionaries in this paper. *(p.7)*
- Simplifications remain: definitions are treated as unordered strings of content words, stopping words and many syntactic details are excluded, and only the first meaning of each word is used. *(p.6-7)*
- Some structural claims may be properties of observed dictionary graphs rather than arbitrary directed graphs in general. *(p.10-11 n.4)*

## Arguments Against Prior Work
- It is not enough to talk about a single grounding set or a single kernel; the different hidden components have distinct functional roles. *(p.1-3)*
- A purely concrete grounding story is too simple: some of the words needed for full lexical power may be more abstract Satellite words. *(p.5-6)*
- Looking only at aggregate dictionary-level psycholinguistic averages hides the internal role structure of the kernel. *(p.3-6)*

## Design Rationale
- Distinguish `Core` from `Kernel` because the Core alone does not define the whole dictionary. *(p.2-3)*
- Distinguish `MGS` from both `Core` and `Kernel` because minimality and centrality are different properties. *(p.2-3)*
- Use mini-dictionaries because large dictionaries obscure the role of individual words inside hidden structures. *(p.7-9)*
- Treat hidden graph structure as functionally meaningful rather than only mathematically convenient. *(p.3-6)*

## Testable Properties
- Every large dictionary graph should contain a small Kernel and a still smaller family of overlapping MGSs. *(p.1-3, p.7)*
- The Core alone should not ground the whole dictionary, whereas any MGS should. *(p.2-3)*
- Hidden graph components should differ psycholinguistically from the rest of the dictionary. *(p.3-5)*
- The Satellite layer should be where simple “more inward means more concrete” stories begin to fail. *(p.4-6)*
- Mini-dictionaries should reproduce at least some of the same hidden structure seen in full dictionaries. *(p.7-9)*

## Relevance to Project
This paper matters because it warns against flattening the kernel into a single seed notion. If we build code for real English kernel extraction, we should compute at least `Kernel`, `Core`, `Satellites`, and candidate `MGS/MinSets`, not just one “core vocabulary.” It also points toward the kind of weighted objective you care about: not just minimum size, but role within the hidden structure. *(p.2-7)*
