# The Latent Structure of Dictionaries

## One-Sentence Summary
The paper models dictionaries as directed graphs from defining words to defined words, shows that they contain a latent structure consisting of a `Kernel`, `Core`, `Satellites`, and many `MinSets`, and reports that these substructures align with psycholinguistic gradients in frequency, age of acquisition, and concreteness. *(p.1-31)*

## Problem Addressed
The paper asks how many words, and which ones, must already be grounded in order for all other dictionary words to be learnable by recursive definition alone. It also asks whether the latent graph-theoretic components of dictionaries differ in psychologically meaningful ways. *(p.1, p.4-7)*

## Key Contributions
- Formalizes dictionaries as directed graphs whose vertices are word meanings and whose arcs point from defining words to defined words. *(p.4-5)*
- Defines and computes the `Kernel`, `Core`, `Satellites`, and `MinSets` for four English dictionaries plus mini-dictionaries generated by an online game. *(p.3-8)*
- Introduces two definitional-distance hierarchies: `K-hierarchy` from the Kernel outward to the Rest, and `C-hierarchy` from the Core outward through the Satellite layer. *(p.6, p.17-20)*
- Shows that the Kernel is about 8-12% of full dictionaries, the Core and Satellites partition the Kernel, and MinSets are about 1% of the full dictionary. *(p.1, p.5-7, p.9)*
- Reports that Core words are typically more frequent and acquired earlier than the Rest, while Satellites are more concrete than the Core; MinSet Core words are younger/more concrete/more frequent than random Core words, and MinSet Satellite words are older/more abstract/less frequent than random Satellite words. *(p.10-16, p.21-23)*
- Interprets these results through a dual-code grounding view: words in a MinSet are candidates for direct grounding, while the rest can in principle be learned definitionally from them. *(p.24-31)*

## Study Design (empirical papers)
- **Type:** Graph-theoretic corpus analysis of four lexicographic resources, linked to psycholinguistic databases. *(p.4-6, p.11)*
- **Population:** Four English dictionaries: Cambridge International Dictionary of English (47,147 word-meanings), Longman Dictionary of Contemporary English (69,223), Merriam-Webster (248,466), and WordNet (132,477), using first meanings only per stemmatized part of speech. *(p.4-7, p.9)*
- **Intervention(s):** Graph reduction, SCC decomposition, MinSet computation/approximation, and comparison against frequency, concreteness, and age-of-acquisition norms. *(p.5-7, p.11-23)*
- **Comparator(s):** Core vs Satellites vs Rest, and MinSet components vs equal-sized random samples from Core and Satellites. *(p.10-16, p.21-23)*
- **Primary endpoint(s):** Sizes of latent graph structures and pairwise psycholinguistic differences among them. *(p.1, p.7-16)*
- **Secondary endpoint(s):** Gradients across definitional distance levels and part-of-speech distributions. *(p.17-23)*
- **Follow-up:** Not longitudinal; analyses are static over dictionary graphs. *(p.4-31)*

## Methodology
Each dictionary is converted into a digraph whose arcs point from every content word in a definition to the word being defined. Undefined words are removed iteratively until no further removal is possible; the remaining subgraph is the `Kernel`. Strongly connected components of the Kernel are then computed. The largest source SCC is called the `Core`, and the other source-connected subgraphs in the Kernel are called `Satellites`. A `MinSet` is a minimum feedback vertex set restricted to the definitional graph so that once those words are assumed known, the remaining graph becomes acyclic and recursively learnable. The authors compute exact MinSets for the small dictionaries and approximate ones for the large dictionaries using an integer linear program solved with CPLEX. They then compare graph components against SUBTLEX frequency, Brysbaert et al. concreteness ratings, and Kuperman et al. age-of-acquisition ratings. *(p.4-7, p.11-16, p.21-23)*

## Key Equations / Statistical Models

$$
\mathrm{Def}(U)=\{u \in V : \text{all predecessors of } u \text{ belong to } U \text{ or are definable from } U\}
$$
Where: `Def(U)` is the set of words definable from a seed set `U` in dictionary graph `D=(V,A)`. A set `U` is a grounding set if `Def(U) = U \cup V`. *(p.5)*

$$
\mathrm{dist}(u)=0 \text{ if } u \in K,\qquad \mathrm{dist}(u)=1+\max\{\mathrm{dist}(v)\}
$$
Where: in the `K-hierarchy`, `v` ranges over predecessors of `u`, giving definitional distance from the Kernel to the Rest. *(p.6)*

$$
\mathrm{dist}(u)=0 \text{ if } u \text{ is in a source SCC of } D',\qquad \mathrm{dist}(u)=1+\max\{\mathrm{dist}(v)\}
$$
Where: in the `C-hierarchy`, `D'` is the SCC-collapsed graph and `v` ranges over predecessors outside the current SCC, giving distance from the Core through the Satellite layer. *(p.6)*

## Methods & Implementation Details
- Ignore syntax and function words; only content words in definitions become graph nodes/edges. Polysemy is approximated by using the first and most common meaning for each part of speech. *(p.2 n.1, p.4-5)*
- `Kernel` is obtained by iterative removal of words that are not used in any definition until no more words can be removed. *(p.5)*
- `MinSet` is treated as a minimum feedback vertex set problem. The general problem is NP-hard, but the authors solve small dictionaries exactly and large ones approximately with CPLEX. *(p.5-7)*
- `Core` is the union of source SCCs inside the Kernel, effectively the largest source SCC after preprocessing in these graphs; `Satellites` are the rest of the Kernel outside the Core. *(p.5-6)*
- `K-hierarchy` measures distance from the Kernel out into the Rest of the dictionary. `C-hierarchy` measures distance from the Core out through the Satellites. Both are acyclic after the relevant collapse/reduction step. *(p.6, p.17-20)*
- Psycholinguistic annotations come from SUBTLEXus frequency, Brysbaert et al. concreteness norms, and Kuperman et al. age-of-acquisition ratings. Coverage is incomplete for age and concreteness, especially in larger dictionaries. *(p.11-15)*
- To probe causal interpretation, the paper also decorrelates age and concreteness from frequency using residuals and repeats effect-size analyses. *(p.13-16)*
- Parts of speech differ by layer: Satellites have relatively more nouns and adjectives and fewer verbs and adverbs than Core or Rest. *(p.23)*
- Mini-dictionaries generated in the online dictionary game show the same qualitative latent structure as full dictionaries, but with reversed Core/Satellite balance inside MinSets. *(p.8-9, p.31)*

## Results Summary
Across all four dictionaries, the graph is dominated by the `Rest` (about 88-92%), with a much smaller `Kernel` (about 8-12%) that nonetheless generates the rest definitionally. Within the Kernel, the `Core` and `Satellites` behave differently: Core words tend to be more frequent and learned younger than the Rest, while Satellite words are more concrete than the Core but still younger and more frequent than the Rest. MinSets are numerous, non-unique, and about 1% of the dictionary; their Core portion is younger/more concrete/more frequent than random Core words, while their Satellite portion is older/more abstract/less frequent than random Satellite words. Definitional distance analyses show graded patterns, especially from Core outward through the Satellite layer. *(p.7-23)*

## Limitations
- Uses only the first and most common meaning for each word-form/part-of-speech, so polysemy is simplified aggressively. *(p.2 n.1, p.7 n.4)*
- Ignores syntax and function words, treating content-word definitions as subject-predicate style propositions. *(p.2 n.1, p.23-24)*
- Age and concreteness coverage are incomplete, especially for larger dictionaries, and this may indirectly bias some effect sizes through missing low-frequency technical terms. *(p.13-16, p.20)*
- MinSets for large dictionaries are only approximate because exact computation is too expensive. *(p.7 n.4, p.21)*
- The paper argues for grounding-based interpretations but does not directly verify which words in a MinSet are truly sensorimotor-grounded. *(p.24-31)*

## Arguments Against Prior Work
- Purely associative or distributional models miss the special role of direct definitional links and therefore do not directly capture grounding sets. *(p.24)*
- A dictionary graph by itself is still ungrounded unless some MinSet words are connected to referents directly in some other way. *(p.24-26)*
- It is implausible that all meaning is learned by direct sensorimotor grounding; language is adaptive because it lets speakers learn new categories by verbal composition from already grounded words. *(p.29-31)*

## Design Rationale
- Separate the graph-theoretic question of recursive definability from the grounding question, but connect them through a dual-code model. *(p.24-26)*
- Use `MinSets` rather than just `Kernel` when the aim is the smallest possible grounding base, because Kernel words are sufficient but not minimal. *(p.5-7, p.24-26)*
- Distinguish `Core` and `Satellites` because they appear to play different cognitive roles: Core is more frequent/earlier-learned, Satellites more concrete and noun/adjective heavy. *(p.10-23)*
- Study mini-dictionaries from an online game because they may reveal the same latent structure closer to individual mental lexicons than editorial dictionaries do. *(p.8-9, p.23, p.31)*

## Testable Properties
- Every sufficiently large English dictionary graph should contain a reducible Rest and a smaller Kernel after iterative undefinability pruning. *(p.3-7)*
- A minimum grounding set should be much smaller than the Kernel and non-unique. *(p.1, p.5-7, p.21)*
- Core words should be more frequent and learned earlier than Rest words; Satellite words should be more concrete than Core words. *(p.10-16)*
- Within the Satellite layer, increasing definitional distance from the Core should be associated with lower frequency, older age, and greater concreteness. *(p.17-20)*
- The MinSet should contain both Core and Satellite members rather than lying wholly in one subcomponent. *(p.8-9, p.21)*

## Relevance to Project
This is the central operational paper for building an English kernel. It gives the graph model, the target objects (`Kernel`, `Core`, `Satellites`, `MinSet`), the distinction between minimality and sufficiency, and the warning that any kernel extracted from dictionary definitions is still only an internal bootstrap unless some seed words are grounded outside the graph. It also points directly toward your cross-linguistic program by ending on the question of whether the same patterns recur in other languages. *(p.24-31)*
